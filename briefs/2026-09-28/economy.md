The macro stress score holds at +0.00 — Calm, with the macro buffer running at full strength (+0.50) and both underlying axes flat for another week — and the honest story is that the framework is in a quiet holding pattern ahead of two data events that will actually matter. The real economy score remains at +0.00, supported by a [GDPNow Q3 estimate that held at 5.0%](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/data/gdpnow) through its September 25 update and a labor market where [initial jobless claims stayed near 197,000](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ICSA) for the week ending September 19, with the next print not due until October 1. On inflation, the six-month core CPI direction is frozen at -0.10: [core CPI sits at 2.4% year-over-year](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm) with three consecutive 0.3% monthly prints accumulating beneath that trend, and the framework won't update the inflation axis until October 14. The soft/hard data divergence flagged last week has not resolved — consumer sentiment remains near historically weak levels amid concerns about fuel prices and trade tension, while [Bank of America card spending data through August shows total household spending running 4.5% above a year ago](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-september-2026.html), two descriptions of the same economy that still disagree. Tomorrow's [August PCE report at 8:30 AM ET](https://stockmarkethours.org/events/us-pce-release) is the first real test of whether that divergence is closing: consensus expects core PCE up 0.3% on the month, and if that lands alongside any softness in the spending number, it would be an early echo of survey weakness showing up in hard data. The stress score doesn't move on PCE alone — the inflation direction axis runs on core CPI — but a hot core PCE print combined with a spending miss would make the October 14 CPI the most consequential single input this framework has faced since inflation began decelerating.
